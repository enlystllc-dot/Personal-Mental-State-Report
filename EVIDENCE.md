# Mental State OS — evidence notes

This document explains what the evidence supports and how much of it should be generalized to a short, self-guided action.

## 1. Sleep and alertness

**Adult sleep duration.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society consensus recommends that adults sleep at least seven hours per night regularly for health. This is a population-level recommendation, not proof that any individual has a precise seven-hour requirement every night.

Source: Watson et al., *Sleep* (2015). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4434546/

**Restricted sleep and sustained attention.** A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 44 studies testing 2–6 hours of sleep opportunity. One night of restricted sleep increased subjective sleepiness (SMD about 0.99) and impaired sustained attention (slower reaction times and more lapses). Other tested domains did not all show reliable effects, so the finding should be stated specifically rather than as universal cognitive failure.

Source: Wüst et al., *Sleep Medicine Reviews* (2024). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/

**Naps.** CDC/NIOSH guidance notes that brief daytime naps can temporarily improve alertness. Sleep inertia can impair performance for a period after waking, especially after sleep restriction or deeper sleep; do not assume that feeling awake immediately after a nap means full performance has returned.

Source: CDC/NIOSH. https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/05.html

**App application:** pronounced subjective sleepiness takes precedence over productivity, breathing or craving strategies. Short sleep combined with current symptoms recommends reducing cognitive demand and planning actual sleep recovery. The app does not calculate exact sleep debt.

## 2. Microbreaks and cognitive fatigue

A 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis identified 19 records with 22 independent samples (N=2,335). Microbreaks produced small improvements in subjective vigor (d=0.36) and fatigue (d=0.35), but the pooled overall-performance effect was not statistically significant (d=0.16, p=.116). Breaks are therefore a plausible way to reduce felt fatigue; they are not a guaranteed performance enhancer, and highly demanding tasks may require more than ten minutes.

Source: Albulescu et al., *PLOS ONE* (2022). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/

**App application:** a screen-free recovery break is recommended for high self-reported fatigue, poor clarity combined with fatigue, or a long work stretch with fatigue. The result is checked afterward.

## 3. Breathwork and stress

A 2023 meta-analysis assessed 12 randomized trials (N=785) for subjective stress and found a small-to-medium average effect (Hedges' g=-0.35). The authors reported moderate risk of bias in many studies and advised caution. Findings support trying comfortable slow breathing for stress, not claims that any precise inhale/exhale ratio normalizes hormones or neurotransmitters.

Source: Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports* (2023). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36624160/

**App application:** a gentle 3-second inhale and 5–6-second exhale for up to five minutes is a low-risk action for a user who reports high stress/physical restlessness. Stop if dizzy or uncomfortable. This specific timing is a practical choice rather than the uniquely proven optimal protocol.

## 4. Mindfulness and attention

A meta-analysis of 34 studies (N=3,524) on brief mindful-attention induction found a small overall effect across cognitive domains (Hedges' g=0.18), with methodological limitations noted. Another meta-analysis of structured mindfulness-based interventions included 111 RCTs (N=9,538) and found small-to-moderate benefits in several cognitive subdomains, but did not find improvements on every endpoint. Repeated programs and a single five-minute session are not equivalent.

Sources:
- Gill et al. (2020): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32739799/
- Bédard / Zainal et al. (published 2023; issue 2024): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37578065/

**App application:** meditation is offered as a short experiment for moderate mental noise, not as a guaranteed instant cognitive reset. The user's own before/after report matters.

## 5. Physical activity and cognition

A 2025 meta-review of 30 systematic reviews/meta-analyses (383 unique studies; N=18,347) reported a small-to-medium average benefit of acute exercise for several cognitive domains (average SMD about 0.33). A separate 2025 umbrella review included 133 systematic reviews and found benefits for cognition, memory and executive function across the broader exercise literature. The broader results do not prove that every five-minute walk produces an immediate measurable benefit.

Sources:
- Chang et al., *Psychological Bulletin* (2025): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39883421/
- Singh et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (2025): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40049759/

**App application:** brief safe movement is offered for high physical sluggishness, with expected benefit framed as possible and variable.

## 6. Dopamine, reward-cue pull and “wanting”

A foundational review by Berridge and Robinson distinguished incentive salience or “wanting” from pleasure or “liking,” and discussed the contribution of dopamine systems to cue-triggered motivation. This is a mechanistic account informed by preclinical and neuroscience evidence; it does not provide a mapping from a person's self-reported urge to their moment-to-moment dopamine concentration.

Sources:
- Berridge & Robinson (1998): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9858756/
- Berridge & Robinson (2009 review): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17072591/

**App application:** the app reports a *behavioral reward-cue pull signal* based on the user's urge rating. It never labels dopamine high/low or calculates a “dopamine level.” Learned cues, novelty, task difficulty, boredom, stress, sleep loss and habit remain alternative explanations.

## 7. What this evidence does not validate

No cited research validates Mental State OS's exact readiness formula, sleep-recovery weighting, rating cutoffs or rule order. The decision system is an explicit, practical heuristic built to prefer low-cost actions with a reasonable evidence base while routing pronounced sleepiness and health signals ahead of productivity. Personal reassessments can inform individual preference, but they cannot by themselves prove causation or reveal neurochemical levels.
